Where this codex disagrees with earlier design notes, the source wins
  • There is no "Weapon Mastery" stat. Wild encounters are gated by a single account-wide World Resonance value; map regions are gated separately by per-element Exploration Level from equipped gear. See Chapter 4.

Part I
Mechanics

01 The Core Loop

Dawn's Journey runs on one loop repeated at every scale, from a single footstep to a whole region: explore the world on foot, discover whatever the tile you land on holds — a creature, a character, a resource, a scripted moment — overcome it by clearing whatever check it presents, acquire the result, and come away able to go further, because almost everything you gain either raises your combat power or unlocks somewhere you couldn't reach before.

There is no separate "combat game" bolted onto an "exploration game" — wild creatures resolve as an instant threshold check rather than a fight (Chapter 5), and the only opponents you actually duel turn-by-turn are named characters and the Arena roster (Chapter 6). Progression is exploration.

02 Interaction Model

Everything interactive resolves through a single floating panel — the Event Screen — that updates every step and surfaces one or two contextual actions instead of a fixed menu. The effect is a controller-friendly, two-input feel: an Interact action whose meaning follows what you're facing, and a Contextual Action whose icon and label change to match it.

  • Facing a character: Talk, plus Challenge (start a duel) and Gift when you're carrying something relevant.
  • Facing a chest, door, or similar object: Open.
  • Facing a resource node: Gather.
  • Inside a scripted scene or any menu: Confirm, or a labelled choice if the moment branches.

03 Exploration & the Event Screen

Movement is tile-based — every input advances the player exactly one tile in a cardinal direction (arrow keys/WASD on desktop, a D-Pad on mobile). Each attempted step is checked, in order, against map boundaries and unwalkable terrain, then a region gate if the destination has one (Chapter 4), and only then resolves against that region's random tables. A resolved step lands on exactly one outcome:

OutcomeWhat happens
NothingThe Event Screen shows the current region's atmospheric art. No mechanical effect.
Enemy EncounterA wild creature is here — resolved instantly, see Chapter 5.
Item FoundA random item is added straight to inventory; the possible pool depends on the region.
CharacterA character's card appears with Talk / Challenge / Gift options.
Location EventA scripted moment plays — dialogue, sometimes a branching choice, sometimes a reward or a lasting change to the tile.
Resource NodeA gathering spot you can harvest directly from the panel — see Chapter 17.

Most scripted Location Events fire once and leave an inert, changed version of the scene behind; a few are marked reusable (a shrine you can rest at repeatedly) and stay live indefinitely.

04 World Resonance & Exploration Level

Two independent stats gate two different kinds of progress.

World Resonance

World Resonance is a single, account-wide number with no elemental split. It climbs passively over real time (it accrues even while you're not playing and is claimed on your next load), from quest rewards, and from a one-time bonus the very first time you defeat any given creature type. It's compared directly against a creature's own Resonance Requirement to decide the win/lose check in Chapter 5 — nothing else about the fight matters.

Exploration Level

Exploration Level is elemental and comes from equipped gear — chiefly Relics, occasionally a Weapon — each carrying bonus levels in one or more elements. A gated region lists one or more alternative (element, level) requirements, and satisfying any single one of them is enough to pass — the "you need Water Exploration to swim here" mechanic runs on this stat, not on World Resonance or on overall combat power.

Note

Because the two systems are independent, it's entirely possible to be strong enough to defeat everything in a region and still be turned back at its border for lacking the right element in your gear — or the reverse.

05 Wild Encounters

Wild creatures are not fought turn-by-turn. Landing on one triggers a single automatic check: if your current World Resonance meets or exceeds that creature's Resonance Requirement, you win outright — the game awards XP, registers a kill (relevant to some quests), rolls the creature's drop table, plays a flavour line chosen by the element of your currently equipped weapon, and, the very first time you've ever beaten that specific creature, grants a one-time World Resonance bonus on top. Falling short means an automatic loss instead: a small fixed HP cost, no rewards, and the creature simply disappears — there is no partial credit and no player input in either outcome.

Legendary creatures run the identical mechanic at a much higher Resonance Requirement, but — unlike ordinary wild creatures — carry a full combat stat block of their own and exclusive drops, and several double as one-off sighting Achievements (Chapter 14).

06 Turn-Based Combat

Actual turn-based combat is reserved for characters: duels against story NPCs (tied to their Sword Rank track, Chapter 10) and the dedicated Arena roster (Data Codex, Section B). The shape of a fight:

  • Speed decides who acts first each round.
  • On your turn you pick one Ability; an AI-controlled opponent picks its own via the phase/trigger system below.
  • Landing a hit clears two independent checks: the ability's own accuracy roll, and a contest between the attacker's Precision and the defender's Evasion (Precision reduces effective Evasion point-for-point). A guaranteed_hit status — the payoff of Lock-On-style setup abilities — skips both, once.
  • A landed hit can additionally roll Critical: a chance stat for whether it happens, a damage stat for how much extra it does.
  • Aura Shield, where present, is a secondary pool that absorbs damage ahead of HP.
  • Elemental effectiveness follows a four-element cycle plus a paired opposition: Fire is strong against Earth, Earth against Wind, Wind against Water, and Water against Fire — and each element resists an attack of its own kind. Light and Dark counter each other directly. Both Light and Dark deal reduced damage against Normal-element targets, while Normal itself carries no advantage or weakness against anything.
  • The fight ends the moment either side's HP reaches zero.

How opponents decide

Every NPC and Arena opponent runs on a phase-and-trigger AI. Their ability roster is split into HP-threshold phases (each played either as a fixed sequence or drawn randomly), overlaid with priority-ordered triggers that fire on specific conditions — the player's HP crossing a line, a status appearing or lapsing on either side, a fixed turn number, an immunity flag being detected on the player. This is what lets an opponent open with a signature move, adapt mid-fight to what you've done, and pivot to a finisher once you're low — see the Combat Archetypes catalog for what this produces in practice.

07 Abilities & Effects

An Ability is a named, targetable action built from one or more Effects that resolve in order. Every ability carries an abilityType — Physical Damage, Elemental Damage, or Special — used for UI grouping, and a stricter category — damage / heal / buff / debuff / control / utility — that reflects its dominant purpose and feeds AI and set-bonus logic. Open-vocabulary tags (an element, a role like finisher/opener/setup/sustain, a synergy marker like poison_apply/poison_payoff) let gear passives, Sets, and dual-weapon bonuses apply targeted damage multipliers without hardcoding ability lists.

Eleven effect types cover everything an ability can do:

  • Damage — physical or elemental damage scaled off the attacker's stats; optionally multi-hit.
  • Conditional Damage — a multiplier that changes based on a condition (an HP threshold on either side, or a status being present) — the basis of "execute" abilities.
  • Apply Status — attaches a Status Effect (Chapter 8) to a target, either replacing or stacking with an existing instance.
  • Stat Modifier — a lighter-weight temporary stat change for straightforward buffs and debuffs.
  • Shield Manipulate — reduces, breaks, or drains an Aura Shield.
  • Heal — restores HP or Aura Shield, scaled off a chosen stat.
  • Stat Transfer — steals a stat from the defender into a (possibly different) stat on the attacker; the defender can retain a portion of what's taken.
  • Heal % Max HP and Heal Full — flat-percentage and full restores, used by "second wind" and resurrection-style abilities.
  • Lifesteal — damage that returns a fraction of what it deals as healing.
  • Cleanse — strips either negative effects (debuffs, poisons, stuns) or positive ones (buffs — functioning as Dispel) from a target.

08 Status Effects, Flags & Tags

A Status Effect is the runtime object abilities create: a duration, an optional damage- or heal-per-turn tick, an optional stun (skips the holder's next turn), optional temporary stat modifiers, and a category — poison / stun / bleed / burn / freeze / buff / debuff — that immunities key off. Gear-granted passives use the identical structure but are marked permanent and immune to being cleansed.

A short set of flags models immunities and one-shot combat guarantees: immunity to stat reduction, immunity to stat increase, immunity to the loss-half of a stat transfer, immunity to poison, immunity to stun, and guaranteed_hit (the carrier's next attack skips both accuracy and evasion, then is consumed). Bleed, burn, and freeze immunity flags already exist in the data model but aren't granted by anything shipped yet — reserved for future status categories.

09 Equipment

Two equipment groups: up to two Weapons and up to four Relics, both contributing combat stats directly. Weapons and some Relics additionally carry Exploration Level bonuses (Chapter 4) and occasionally a Gear Passive — a small always-on effect (an immunity, a tag-based damage bonus, a damage-over-time multiplier) baked in as a permanent status the instant the item is equipped.

Sets are predefined groups of specific Relics; wearing enough pieces from the same set unlocks an escalating bonus at each piece-count threshold — flat stats, a bonus conditional on an equipped weapon's element, a tag-targeted damage multiplier, a damage-over-time multiplier, or a bonus that only applies while the wearer is faster or slower than their opponent (re-evaluated every single strike, so it can flip mid-fight). Separately, equipping two weapons that share an element unlocks its own dual-weapon bonus — a standing incentive to commit to one element rather than mixing.

A handful of preset loadouts — quick-swap combinations such as a physical glass-cannon build or an Aura-shield tank build — exist purely as convenience presets for jumping into a playstyle without re-equipping six items by hand.

10 Sword Rank & Heart Rank

Every combat-capable character tracks two independent, numeric relationship ladders. Sword Rank rises through duelling and defeating them — some characters weight this far more heavily than any gift. Heart Rank rises through conversation, time, and Gifting (Chapter 11), gating its own dialogue, rewards, and story content with its own rank-up requirements and rewards, entirely separate from the Sword track. The two can move independently — it's possible to earn someone's combat respect long before they warm up to you, or the other way around.

11 Gifting

From the Event Screen, a character with a defined gift list can be handed an item straight out of inventory. Each item that character actually recognises has its own short reaction line and its own contribution toward their Heart Rank — recognised gifts aren't worth the same amount, so the system rewards knowing what a specific character wants rather than just spending items at them. Handing over something outside their list plays no reaction and has no relationship effect. See Section H of the Data Codex for the underlying data shape.

12 Quests

A quest is a Rank: a title, a description, an optional start requirement, and an ordered list of Stages. Each stage has an objective, a Requirement that must be satisfied to clear it, and optional dialogue for when the stage begins, when it's reminded, and when it succeeds — plus success rewards, and, for stages that allow it, a refusal path with its own dialogue and effects.

13 Factions & Reputation

Two structurally different faction layers exist side by side. At the top sits a single binary allegiance between two large, opposed powers — a devout, order-preserving public institution and its secretive, unseen counterpart — which shapes certain quest and reward branches project-wide once chosen. Beneath that, a set of smaller, local factions each track their own independent Score and tiered Rank, raised through quests, character interactions, and world events, each unlocking its own item rewards at set score thresholds. Some local factions are explicit rivals: gaining Score with one automatically docks a fraction of it from the rival.

14 Achievements

Achievements fall into four categories: Sighting (first encounter with a specific — often legendary — creature), Kill Count (cumulative defeats, single- or multi-tier), Collection, and Milestone. Some are marked secret and stay hidden until unlocked. Multi-tier achievements track progress toward successive thresholds, each awarding its own reward independently.

15 Homestead: Farming

The Homestead holds the player's personal farm plots, organised into unlockable Environments — an always-available open field, plus gated ones like a greenhouse or a forest floor — each starting with a base plot count that can be expanded further through the Workshop (Chapter 19). Each plot holds one Crop at a time, planted from a matching Seed. A crop advances through several timed Growth Stages — real-world-clock timers that keep running even while the game is closed — each optionally gated by a watering requirement in one of two flavours: lifetime-based (water it enough times, ever, across the whole grow) or stage-based (water it enough times since the current stage began). A stage whose timer has finished but whose watering hasn't been met is flagged and stalls until watered. Most crops also carry an Ideal Season, in which they grow faster and yield more. Harvesting yields the crop item — sometimes with a secondary "leaves" by-product — plus Farming XP; some crops additionally require a specific Environment and/or a specific unlocked Workshop technology before they can be planted at all.

16 Composting

Leaves gathered while harvesting convert into Compost — a soil amendment some crops require — by committing them, in fixed batches, to a timed composting queue that runs down in real time exactly like crop growth; larger batches simply take proportionally longer.

17 Gathering Skills

Woodcutting and Mining are the pure gathering skills, worked through Resource Nodes scattered across the world — trees and logs yielding wood, rock and ore veins yielding stone and similar materials. Every node defines a required skill level to attempt, a fixed reward, a cooldown before it can be gathered again, a cap on total gathers before it's exhausted for good, and its own XP-per-gather value. Alchemy gathers some of its own raw ingredients the same way (gem fountains, mushroom patches) in addition to its production role below.

18 Production & Crafting

Three skills turn gathered or farmed materials into new items. Smithing forges Weapons from raw materials. Cooking turns ingredients into dishes that heal and/or grant temporary combat buffs — several recipes additionally consume Time Point (Chapter 21) as an ingredient, standing in for real preparation time. Alchemy brews Potions — temporary stat elixirs — from herbs, gems, and crops. Every recipe shares the same shape: a skill, a list of ingredient items and quantities, an output item and quantity, an XP award, and a minimum skill level. A parallel materials track under Smithing turns raw gems plus a secondary ingredient into elemental Bars, which feed into weapon crafting and upgrades of that element.

19 The Workshop

The Workshop is a Farming-linked tech tree, spent in Tech Points earned by levelling the Farming skill. Its nodes unlock new farm Environments, expand an existing environment's plot count, or unlock supporting technologies (raised beds, vertical supports, cold frames, and similar) that specific crops require before they can be planted. Nodes can carry level requirements and prerequisite nodes, occasionally offering alternative (either/or) prerequisites.

20 Player Progression

Beyond the six skills, each with its own level/XP curve and per-level rewards, the player character has an overall Level, gaining XP from combat and other sources and awarding Level-Up Points spent permanently on individual combat stats. World Resonance (Chapter 4) is a separate, always-climbing axis with no "level" of its own — it's a raw accumulating number checked directly against enemy requirements.

21 Economy & Shops

Argentum is the primary currency — earned from world drops and quest rewards, spent in shops and some crafting. Time Point is a secondary, passively-accumulating currency (earned simply by playing) spent almost exclusively as a Cooking ingredient. Shops present three separate storefronts: a Café selling prepared dishes, a Grocery selling raw ingredients and everyday goods, and a curated rarities counter for a handful of special items priced well above the rest.

22 The Journal

An in-game reference — chapters for Achievements, Characters, Enemies, a Glossary, a How-to-Play index, and Items — mirrors much of what's documented here, browsable at any time without leaving the game.

23 Saves & Profiles

The game autosaves the full player state — inventory, position, quest/achievement/faction progress, farm plots, and more. Beyond a standard fresh start, named starting Profiles exist, including a curated "everything unlocked" exhibition mode for free exploration of the full item and weapon roster.

B Combat Archetypes

The Arena roster and named duel opponents, described by fighting style rather than identity — drawn from the phase/trigger design of each combatant (Chapter 6).

The Forest Ranger

Patient and reactive. Opens by applying a poison and keeps it topped up all fight, escalating to raw elemental damage once critically wounded. Detects poison immunity on the first exchange and switches entirely to direct damage if it finds one.

The Iron Lioness

Opens by converting its own Attack into Defence, then grinds the opponent down with stuns, debuffs, and poison. Carries two one-time self-heals, each triggered at a specific health threshold.

The Ice Aristocrat

Opens by converting Defence into Attack, then snowballs with periodic attack buffs and defence-shredding debuffs, plus a battlefield chill effect that recurs on a fixed cadence.

The Holy Commander

A burst archetype: a safety/evasion opener, an accuracy-guaranteeing follow-up while healthy, and an all-in execute once the opponent drops low.

The Juggernaut

Permanently immune to both stun and stat reduction — no control or debuff works on it at all. Resurrects itself once when brought near defeat. Wins purely through sustained raw damage; there is no trick that shortcuts the fight.

The Pirate Captain

Pure aggression. Fast, hard-hitting, and largely sequence-driven rather than reactive, escalating to an explosive area-style attack once the opponent is low.

The Sea Sovereign

A bulky, evasive sustain archetype. Opens with a defensive ward, locks a persistent debuff onto the opponent early, periodically ascends for a temporary power spike, and leans on a healing/damage hybrid attack when hurt.

The Tidal Duelist

Runs the Sea Sovereign's exact ability kit and opening sequence, but rebuilt as a leaner, far faster mirror of it — trading the Sovereign's bulk for extreme speed, evasion, and precision.

The Berserker

Stacks a self-buff every single turn without exception, carries no healing or utility of its own, and grows disproportionately dangerous the longer a fight against it runs. Abandons its normal pattern entirely for a harder-hitting, all-out mode once critically wounded.

The Lifestealer

Its own damage output scales with its remaining HP, so its whole strategy is staying topped up — a regenerating aura, lifesteal, and a cleanse-plus-heal panic button when hurt, plus a Dispel to punish the opponent's own buffs.

The Punisher

A pure counter-archetype. Raises its own defence the moment the opponent buffs their attack, ignores the opponent's defence buffs and hits straight through them, strips healing-over-time and precision-lock effects on sight, and punishes a guaranteed-hit setup by striking first before it can land.

The Trickster

Built entirely around very high Evasion backed by high Precision, so the opponent's own Evasion barely helps against it in return. Opens by stacking evasion immediately and keeps a precision debuff on the opponent topped up — only a guaranteed-hit effect reliably gets a clean hit in. This exact kit appears twice in the roster, on two separate combatants.

H Gifting Data

The underlying shape of a Gift, as defined per-character (see Chapter 11 for how the mechanic plays out).

FieldMeaning
Item & QuantityThe exact item and amount that must be handed over in one gift.
ValueHow much Heart Rank progress this specific gift is worth — varies per item, not a flat amount per gift.
Reaction DialogueA short line or two of flavour text the character responds with, unique to that item.

A handful of Grocery items exist chiefly as gift-appropriate curios rather than cooking ingredients — a handwritten note, heart-shaped chocolates, a rare perfume — suited to being handed to a character rather than consumed or used in a recipe. Only items on a character's own defined gift list register any reaction or Heart Rank progress; anything else can still be handed over narratively but has no mechanical effect.
